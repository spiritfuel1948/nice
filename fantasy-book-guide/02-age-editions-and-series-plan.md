# One World, Three Shelves: Kids, Teens, and Adults

You asked for options for **children, teens, and adults**, so you have more ways
to earn depending on how the book does. Here's what the research says and how
I'd do it.

---

## The honest truth first

**You can't sell one manuscript to all three ages.** Each age group needs:
- a **different-aged hero** (kids want heroes a little older than themselves),
- a **different length** (about 600 words for a picture book, 90,000+ for an
  adult novel), and
- **different content limits** (romance, violence, language, darkness).

Rewriting one story three times is three times the work, and each version
comes out weaker.

**What works instead: share the *world*, not the *manuscript*.** You build one
world bible (magic, knights, lore, map) once. Then you write **separate
stories** set in that world, each built for its audience. Your world-building
work pays off three times, and each book is strong for its readers.

```
                       ┌──────────────────────┐
                       │   YOUR STORY BIBLE   │
                       │ magic · knights · map│
                       │ lore · history       │
                       └──────────┬───────────┘
          ┌───────────────────────┼───────────────────────┐
          ▼                       ▼                       ▼
  KIDS (ages 6–9)         TEENS (13–17, YA)          ADULTS (18+)
  Early chapter books     Novella series             Novella series
  younger heroes,         teen heroes, real          adult heroes, darker
  episodic adventures     stakes, first love         or romantasy, gray morals
```

## The age categories, side by side

Word counts vary by source, so treat them as ranges, not rules. Fantasy usually
runs at the long end because of worldbuilding.
([CANSCAIP](https://canscaip.org/Childrens-Book-Categories),
[SelfPublishing.com](https://selfpublishing.com/?p=42456),
[Writer's Digest](https://writersdigest.com/write-better-fiction/the-key-differences-between-middle-grade-vs-young-adult))

| Category | Reader age | Hero age | Length | Content limits | What it costs you to make |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Picture book** | 3–7 | any (often animals or young kids) | 400–800 words, 32 pages | Gentle; a problem solved | **High:** $2,000–6,000 for illustrations, plus fixed-layout formatting |
| **Early chapter book** | 6–9 | ~7–10 | 4,000–15,000 words | Mild peril, humor, friendship; no romance | **Medium:** cover plus some interior art helps (e.g., Scholastic's *Dragon Masters*, about 90 illustrated pages) |
| **Middle grade (MG)** | 8–12 | 10–13 | 20,000–55,000 (fantasy up to ~60,000) | Crushes at most; violence mostly magical or monster-based; mild language | **Low:** cover only |
| **Young adult (YA)** | 13–17 (plus many adults) | 14–18 | 50,000–90,000 for novels (fantasy often longer); **novellas work for series** | Romance OK; real violence and darker themes OK; moderate language | **Low:** cover only |
| **Adult** | 18+ | adult | Novellas 17,500–40,000; novels often 90,000–120,000+ | No limits (just label them clearly) | **Low:** cover only |

**Details from the research:**
- **Middle grade vs. YA:** Middle-grade heroes are usually 10–13 and stories
  focus on the hero's immediate world. YA heroes are 14–18 and wrestle with
  identity and their place in the wider world. A college-age hero usually
  pushes a book into "new adult." One author who writes both advises **avoiding
  a 13-year-old main character** because publishers don't know where to shelve
  the book.
  ([Writer's Digest](https://writersdigest.com/write-better-fiction/the-key-differences-between-middle-grade-vs-young-adult),
  [The Write Life](https://thewritelife.com/young-adult-vs-middle-grade-fiction/))
- **Adults buy YA too:** A Bowker study reported by *Publishers Weekly* found
  that **55% of YA book buyers were 18 or older**, and 78% of the time they
  were buying for themselves. The study is from 2012, but YA crossover has
  only grown since.
  ([Publishers Weekly](https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/industrynews/publisher-news/article/53937-new-study-55-of-ya-books-bought-by-adults.html))
- **Amazon's age settings:** KDP's "Reading Age" bands are **0–2, 3–5, 6–8,
  9–12** (Children's categories) and **13–17** (Teen & Young Adult). Without a
  reading age, a kids' or teen book may not be eligible for those categories or
  appear in age-filtered search.
  ([KDP: Primary Audience and Reading Age](https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G201506310))
- **Picture books are the hardest to self-publish.** They need professional
  illustrations: roughly $75–250 per page professionally, and about
  $2,000–6,000 for a 32-page book
  ([Kidillus](https://kidillus.com/learn/childrens-book-illustration-cost),
  [Reedsy](https://blog.reedsy.com/guide/how-to-publish-a-childrens-book/cost/)).
  They also need **fixed-layout** ebooks. Amazon retired *Kindle Kids' Book
  Creator*; you now use **Kindle Create**
  ([Vappingo](https://www.vappingo.com/word-blog/kdp-children-book-formatting/)).
  And if you use AI art, Amazon requires you to **disclose** it.

## My recommendation: launch order

### 1st: Teens (YA) novella series that adults also read
**Why start here:**
- **Cheapest to launch.** No illustrations; you only need a cover.
- **Two audiences at once.** Teens *and* the adults who buy YA.
- **The format fits.** Novellas of 20,000–30,000 words are short enough to
  finish and long enough to feel like a real book. Ebooks plus Kindle Unlimited
  work well for series readers (see the publishing guide).
- **Flexible.** "PG-13" content can later be aged *down* for kids or *up* for
  adults.
- **Knights and wizards fit naturally.** Coming of age, oaths, first loyalty,
  first love, finding out the adults were wrong.

### 2nd: Kids' early chapter books (ages 6–9), a spin-off
- Same world, **younger heroes**. For example: a squire and an apprentice
  wizard, each about 9, or your YA hero's little sibling.
- **Episodic:** each book is a complete adventure of 6,000–12,000 words that
  kids can read in any order.
- Use the **Story Spine** (Lesson 9) as your outline. It's ideal for this length.
- Budget for a strong cover and, if possible, a few interior illustrations.
- Parents and teachers do much of the buying at this age, so these books need
  a **paperback**, not just an ebook. *(My reasoning; check comparable
  bestsellers.)*

### 3rd: Adults, a darker side series or a romantasy
- Same world, a **different era or region**. For example: the war 30 years
  before the YA series, or the story of the YA hero's mentor when they were
  young.
- This is where you can go darker (moral grayness, real consequences) or lean
  into **romantasy**, which 2026 trend reports name as fantasy's biggest growth
  area ([PublishDrive](https://publishdrive.com/romantasy-book-trends.html)).
- **Keep it clearly separate from the kids' books.** Use a different series
  name and cover style, and consider a **different pen name**. KDP allows pen
  names, but the author name **can't be changed after publishing**, and
  features like Author Pages depend on keeping it consistent
  ([KDP: Authors & Contributors](https://kdp.amazon.com/en_US/help/topic/G2BWJN2BY98T5PV2)).
  Parents shouldn't stumble from a kids' book into an adult romance through
  your author page.

> **If you'd rather go adult first** (e.g., romantasy, because it's the hottest
> market), that's a valid choice. Just know that romantasy readers expect a
> central romance with a happy ending. It's a promise to readers, not a
> decoration.

## Splitting the series into parts (YA example)

**"Season 1": 4 novellas of about 25,000 words each, about 100,000 words total**

| Part | Closes this thread (satisfying) | Leaves this open (keep reading) |
|---|---|---|
| **Part 1** | An **Inquiry**, e.g., a mystery solved | The **Event** (the big threat) is revealed |
| **Part 2** | A **Milieu**, e.g., escaping a dangerous place | A **Character** crisis (the hero's Lie gets worse) |
| **Part 3** | An Inquiry or Milieu | **All Is Lost** for the season arc |
| **Part 4** | **Everything**: the season's Event and the hero's Character arc | A small hook for Season 2 |

**Then:** combine Parts 1–4 into a **Season 1 Omnibus** ebook and paperback.
It's a "new" product that sells to readers who prefer one big book. On
paperbacks, an omnibus is also where the money is (math in the publishing
guide).

**Release timing (my recommendation):**
- **Write Parts 1 and 2 before you publish Part 1.** You'll never leave
  readers waiting long, and you can plant clues in Part 1 that pay off later.
- Then release a part every 4–8 weeks, depending on your writing speed.
- Amazon's **2-new-titles-per-format-per-week** limit (since September 2026)
  won't affect this pace
  ([Vappingo](https://www.vappingo.com/word-blog/kdp-publishing-limit/)).

**Kids' chapter book series:** No season arc is needed. Make each book
standalone with the same heroes, like *Magic Tree House* or *Dragon Masters*.
Number them anyway (Book 1, 2, 3…) so they link on Amazon's series page.

## Which option earns what? (Rough per-copy math)

These are illustrative. Real numbers depend on your exact price, file size,
print cost, and current Amazon rates. The full math is in
[`03-publishing-and-money.md`](03-publishing-and-money.md).

| Product | Example price | You earn per copy (approx.) |
|---|---|---|
| YA/adult novella ebook | $2.99 | ~$2.03 (70% minus a small delivery fee) |
| YA/adult novella ebook | $0.99 (launch promo) | ~$0.35 (35%) |
| Season omnibus ebook (4 parts) | $7.99 | ~$5.44 |
| Season omnibus paperback (~330 pages) | $15.99 | ~$4.59 (60% minus ~$5 print cost) |
| Kids' chapter book ebook | $2.99 | ~$2.03 |
| Kindle Unlimited reads | paid per page read | changes monthly (~$0.0048/page in 2026, estimated by third-party trackers) |
