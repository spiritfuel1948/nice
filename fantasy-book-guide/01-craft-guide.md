## Lesson 1 — Premise and Theme (The Heart of Your Story)

**What it is.** A **premise** is your story in one sentence. A **theme** is
the question your story explores. A theme is a question, not a moral.

**Why it matters.** If you can't say the story in one sentence, you'll get
lost while writing it. The theme is what makes a book *mean* something, and it
connects directly to your hero's arc (Lesson 7).

**How to do it.** Fill in this formula:

> When **[something happens]**, a **[flawed character]** must **[goal]**, or
> else **[stakes]**. But **[complication that makes it hard]**.

*Example (for practice only; your story will be your own):*
> When a disgraced squire is ordered to escort a captured wizard to trial, she
> must deliver him alive or lose her last chance at knighthood. But the longer
> they travel, the more evidence she finds that her own order wants him dead
> before he can talk.

Then turn the conflict into a **theme question**: *Is loyalty to your order
worth more than the truth?* Your story doesn't preach an answer. It *tests*
the question through your hero's choices.

**Exercise 1.** Write **three** different premises using the formula. Use
three different wizard-and-knight setups. Pick the one that excites you most,
even if it's the weirdest. Write its theme as a question.

## Lesson 2 — Worldbuilding (The Iceberg)

**What it is.** Building the invented world your story happens in: its land,
peoples, power, history, and magic.

**Why it matters.** J.R.R. Tolkien argued in his essay *On Fairy-Stories* that
a fantasy writer is a **"sub-creator"** who builds a *Secondary World* the
reader's mind can enter. The key is **"the inner consistency of reality"**:
the world has to follow its own rules. Once the reader stops believing, *"the
spell is broken."*
([commentary](https://nathanbaugh.substack.com/p/tolkiens-on-fairy-stories))

**How to do it.**

1. **Build an iceberg, but only the tip has to be real.** Brandon Sanderson
   teaches that the goal is to *give the illusion of an iceberg*. You don't need
   the whole thing, only enough that readers believe it's there.
   ([Writer's Digest](https://www.writersdigest.com/write-better-fiction/world-building-tips),
   [Sanderson's lecture notes](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/worldbuilding-tools-lecture-2025))
2. **Worldbuilding serves the story, not the other way around.** Build in
   detail what your characters *touch*. Sketch everything else.
3. **Work in three layers:**
   - **Physical:** map, climate, travel times, what people eat, what's scarce
   - **Cultural:** who holds power, religion, classes, laws, taboos, daily life,
     how people insult each other, what they celebrate
   - **Magical:** see Lesson 3
4. **Follow the ripples.** Every big fact changes other things. *If wizards
   can heal, what happens to doctors? If knights rule, who pays for their
   armor?* Asking "and so…?" is how worlds start to feel real.
5. **Expand before you add.** Sanderson's Third Law applies to worlds too:
   deepen what you already have before you invent something new.
   ([Sanderson](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/guide-to-sandersons-laws-of-magic-lecture-notes))

**Exercise 2.** Describe the town or castle where Part 1 begins by answering
these 10 questions:
- What does it smell like?
- Who's in charge?
- Who's poor?
- What do people fear?
- What's the local legend?
- What do people eat?
- How do people get around?
- What's the newest problem?
- Where do knights stand in this place?
- Where do wizards stand in this place?

## Lesson 3 — Magic Systems (Your Wizards)

**What it is.** The rules for how magic works in your world: where it comes
from, who can use it, what it costs, and what it *can't* do.

**Why it matters.** Magic is the main reason readers pick up fantasy, and it's
also the easiest way to break a story. If magic can solve anything, nothing is
at stake.

**How to do it: Sanderson's Laws of Magic.**
([Sanderson's own notes](https://www.brandonsanderson.com/blogs/blog/guide-to-sandersons-laws-of-magic-lecture-notes),
[Coppermind summary](https://coppermind.net/wiki/Sanderson%27s_Laws_of_Magic))

| Law | What it says | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| **Zeroth Law** | *Err on the side of awesome.* | Start from a cool idea that excites you, then build the rules around it. |
| **First Law** | An author's ability to solve conflict with magic is directly proportional to how well the reader understands that magic. | If a wizard saves the day with magic the reader never understood, it feels like cheating (a *deus ex machina*). Teach the rules *before* the big moment. |
| **Second Law** | Limitations are more interesting than powers. | Costs, limits, and weaknesses create drama. Ask what each spell takes from the wizard. |
| **Third Law** | Expand what you already have before you add something new. | One magic ability explored deeply beats twenty shallow ones. |

**Hard vs. soft magic.** These are two ends of a scale:
- **Hard magic** has clear rules the reader learns. Characters can use it to
  solve problems because the reader understands it (First Law).
- **Soft magic** stays mysterious and creates wonder and dread. It should
  *cause* problems, not solve the hero's problems.

Wizards in a knights-and-wizards story often work well **hard-ish**, so readers
understand the rules. Ancient or forbidden magic can stay **soft** and mysterious.

**Magic system worksheet** (it's also in the template):
1. **Source:** Where does the power come from? Words, blood, stars, oaths,
   bloodlines, study, bargains?
2. **Access:** Who can use it? Is it rare, inherited, learned, or stolen?
3. **Cost:** What does each spell take? Energy, memories, years of life, pain,
   a debt?
4. **Limits:** What can magic **never** do?
5. **Weakness:** What stops or counters it? (This is where knights can come in.)
6. **Society:** How does the world treat wizards? Respected, feared, owned,
   licensed, hunted?
7. **Signature:** What does it look, sound, and smell like when someone casts?

*Practice example of linking magic to knights:* "Oath-magic." A wizard's power
comes from promises. A broken promise burns away one of the wizard's memories.
That's why wizards are bound to knights, whose whole culture is built on
keeping oaths. **Come up with your own.** This only shows how one rule can
connect your two groups.

**Exercise 3.** Fill in the 7-question worksheet for your magic. Then write
down **one thing magic absolutely cannot do**. Your story's climax may depend
on that limit.

## Lesson 4 — Knights, Orders, and Oaths

> This lesson is my own synthesis. It combines the worldbuilding research above
> with common genre knowledge. Real history, like chivalric codes and orders
> such as the Templars and Hospitallers, and Arthurian legend, the original
> wizard-and-knight pairing of Merlin and the Round Table, are great
> inspiration to research on your own.

**What it is.** Designing your knights as a *culture*, not just people in armor.

**Why it matters.** "Wizards and knights" gives you a built-in conflict. Knights
usually stand for **duty, strength, loyalty, and tradition**. Wizards usually
stand for **knowledge, power, and the unknown**. The relationship between the
two groups is what makes your series *yours*.

**How to do it.** Answer these questions:
- **The Oath:** What exactly do knights swear? Write the actual words. What
  happens to oath-breakers?
- **The Order(s):** Is there one order or rival orders? Who can become a knight:
  only nobles, or anyone who passes trials?
- **Duty vs. reward:** What do knights owe, and what do they get in return?
- **The Code's crack:** Where does the order fail its own ideals? This is
  where great conflict comes from.
- **Knights vs. wizards:** Choose the default relationship. Then, for a strong
  story, put your heroes *against* that default.

| Relationship | Story it creates |
|---|---|
| **Rivals** | Knights and wizards compete for the king's favor. |
| **Partners** | Each knight is bonded to a wizard, and the pair is a weapon. |
| **Masters and servants** | Wizards are owned or licensed by the orders. |
| **Hunters and hunted** | Knights exist to stop dangerous magic. |
| **Guardians** | Knights protect wizards, who are physically fragile. |

**Exercise 4.** Write your knights' oath (3–6 lines). Then write one sentence
about a way the order secretly breaks it.

## Lesson 5 — Lore and History (Without Info-Dumping)

**What it is.** Your world's past: wars, legends, prophecies, songs, religions,
and fallen kingdoms.

**Why it matters.** Lore makes a world feel old and deep. But dumping it on
readers ("info-dumping") is one of the most common reasons readers stop
reading fantasy.

**How to do it.**
([Savannah Gilbo](https://www.savannahgilbo.com/blog/worldbuilding-without-info-dumping),
[Writers' Fun Zone](https://writersfunzone.com/blog/2026/01/26/world-building-without-information-dumping/))
1. **Show the world through your point-of-view character.** Readers learn what
   the hero notices, in the hero's voice and opinions.
2. **Reveal lore when it matters to the stakes.** Share a legend *right when*
   it threatens or helps the hero.
3. **Rule of thumb: "Less than you think, later than you think."**
4. **Let conflict carry the exposition.** An argument about a law teaches the
   law. Avoid the "As you know, Captain…" problem, where characters explain
   things to each other that they both already know.
5. **Use discovery.** A character visiting somewhere new, or learning a skill,
   lets the reader learn along with them. (An apprentice wizard is perfect for
   this.)
6. **A few vivid sensory details** do more than a page of history.
7. **Make the lore a little wrong.** *(My tip.)* Legends that are partly false,
   or told differently by knights and wizards, turn history into a mystery
   readers want solved. This works especially well for a multi-part series.
8. **Use chapter epigraphs.** *(My tip.)* A short quote from an in-world book,
   song, or oath at the top of each chapter sneaks in lore without slowing the
   story.

**Exercise 5.** Pick one big event from your world's past, like a war or a
betrayal. Write it **three ways**: as knights tell it, as wizards tell it, and
as peasants tell it. The differences between those versions are where story
ideas come from.

## Lesson 6 — Character Development (Want, Need, Wound, Lie)

**What it is.** Building characters who feel like real people, with a past that
explains how they act now.

**Why it matters.** Readers come for the magic and stay for the characters. A
great plot with flat characters gets forgotten. Flawed, memorable characters
get readers to buy the next book.

**How to do it: the backstory chain.**
([K.M. Weiland](https://www.helpingwritersbecomeauthors.com/),
[*The Emotional Wound Thesaurus*](https://writershelpingwriters.net/?p=64344))

```
WOUND ──► LIE ──► FEAR ──► FLAW / DEFENSE ──► WANT  (the goal they chase)
                                                 ✕  clashes with
                                              NEED  (what would actually heal them)
```

- **Wound:** A painful event from the past. *The Emotional Wound Thesaurus*
  (Ackerman & Puglisi) has more than 100 wound types. For each one, it lists
  the false beliefs, fears, coping habits, and triggers the wound tends to
  create.
- **Lie:** What the character believes because of the wound. For example: *"If
  I fail, I'm worthless,"* or *"Magic always corrupts."*
- **Want:** The goal the character *thinks* they need, usually the plot goal.
- **Need:** What would actually fix them. They usually can't see it at first.

**More tools:**
- **Give every main character a contradiction** (a cowardly knight, a gentle
  necromancer), **a secret**, **a skill**, and **a recognizable way of talking**.
- **Supporting characters work best as mirrors.** Each one shows a *different
  answer* to your theme question.
- **The villain believes they're right.** A strong villain often *is* the hero's
  Lie taken to its extreme. They want something understandable and do terrible
  things to get it.

**Exercise 6.** Fill in the character sheet in the template for your hero:
wound, Lie, fear, want, need, contradiction, secret, and voice.

## Lesson 7 — Character Arcs (How People Change)

**What it is.** How a character changes, or refuses to change, from the first
page to the last.

**Why it matters.** K.M. Weiland's *Creating Character Arcs* ties arc to
**theme**. The character starts out believing a **Lie** that seems to protect
them. The story gradually proves it wrong, and they must choose the **Truth**.
([Weiland's blog](https://www.goodreads.com/author/show/2975035.K_M_Weiland/blog?page=41),
[summary](https://www.shortform.com/pdf/creating-character-arcs-pdf-k-m-weiland))

**The three arc types:**

| Arc | What happens | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive change** | The hero lets go of the Lie and embraces the Truth. | Most heroes, and nearly all kids' and YA books |
| **Flat** | The hero *already* knows the Truth. They don't change; they change the world around them. | Mentor wizards, steadfast knights, long-running series heroes |
| **Negative change** | The hero clings to the Lie, or learns a bleak truth ("disillusionment"). | Villains, tragic figures, dark adult fantasy |

**How to map an arc onto a story:**
- **Beginning:** Show the Lie in action. It's working for them… mostly.
- **Middle (midpoint):** The hero glimpses the Truth and starts acting
  differently, but hasn't committed yet.
- **Low point:** The Lie costs them badly.
- **Climax:** They **choose** the Truth (or reject it, in a negative arc), and
  that choice is how they win (or lose).
- **Ending:** A final image that **mirrors the opening** and shows how they've
  changed.
  ([Save the Cat](https://www.jessicabrody.com/2020/11/how-to-write-your-novel-using-the-save-the-cat-beat-sheet/))

**For a multi-part series:** Each part moves the arc **one clear step**. The
full transformation happens over the whole series (one "season").

**Exercise 7.** Write your hero's Lie and Truth as two opposite sentences. Then
write one scene idea where the Lie *helps* them and one where it *hurts* them.

## Lesson 8 — Emotion (Making Readers Feel It)

**What it is.** Getting readers to *feel* what your characters feel, instead of
just being told about it.

**Why it matters.** People recommend books that made them feel something.
Readers want to *experience* emotion themselves, not be told what a character
feels. ([*The Writer* on *The Emotion Thesaurus*](https://www.writermag.com/?p=31087))

**How to do it: show emotion through four channels.**
*The Emotion Thesaurus* (Ackerman & Puglisi) lists the following for each
emotion: body language, internal sensations, and thoughts, from mild to extreme.
It warns that characters in weak drafts "smile, shrug, nod, and frown" far too
much.

| Channel | Example for *fear* |
|---|---|
| **Body (outside)** | Her hand found the sword hilt before she'd decided to draw it. |
| **Visceral (inside)** | Her stomach turned to cold water. |
| **Thoughts** | *He knows. Somehow, he knows.* |
| **Action / dialogue** | "We're leaving. Now." She was already moving. |

*Told:* "Mira was very afraid of the wizard."
*Shown:* "Mira kept the table between herself and the wizard and didn't take
her eyes off his hands."

**Emotion across the whole story.** A 2016 study (Reagan et al., *EPJ Data
Science*) analyzed more than 1,300 novels and found that their emotional arcs
mostly follow **six basic shapes**. The idea was inspired by Kurt Vonnegut's
"shapes of stories."
([paper](https://epjds.epj.org/articles/epjdata/abs/2016/01/13688_2016_Article_93/13688_2016_Article_93.html))

| Shape | Movement | Fantasy example |
|---|---|---|
| Rags to riches | steady rise | humble squire becomes a hero |
| Tragedy (riches to rags) | steady fall | proud wizard destroyed by ambition |
| **Man in a hole** | fall → rise | the classic adventure |
| **Icarus** | rise → fall | power gained, then lost |
| Cinderella | rise → fall → rise | most fairy tales |
| **Oedipus** | fall → rise → fall | dark fantasy |

The study found **Icarus, Oedipus, and two "man in a hole" arcs in a row** were
the most downloaded. That's useful for series: each part can be a "hole" the
hero climbs out of.

**More techniques:**
- **Try/fail cycles.** Heroes should fail before they succeed. Sanderson and
  others teach that each failure should raise the stakes.
- **Stakes + caring + uncertainty = emotion.** Readers have to *care* about the
  person, *know* what could be lost, and *not be sure* it'll work out.
- **End chapters on an emotional turn or a new question.** That's what makes
  readers keep reading.

**Exercise 8.** Take one emotion your hero feels in Part 1. Write it as a
"told" sentence, then rewrite it using at least three of the four channels.

## Lesson 9 — Story Structure (The Skeleton)

**What it is.** The order of the major events that keep a story moving. There
are three levels below: the whole story, the big beats, and individual scenes.

### Level 1: The Story Spine (the simplest tool, and great for kids' books)
Created by playwright and improv teacher **Kenn Adams**. Pixar's story team
later adopted it.
([NPR, 2026](https://www.wunc.org/2026-06-23/meet-the-creator-of-the-story-spine-an-8-sentence-tool-to-create-and-analyze-stories))

> **Once upon a time** … **Every day** … **But one day** … **Because of that** …
> **Because of that** … **Because of that** … **Until finally** … **And ever
> since then** …

The repeated "because of that" forces **cause and effect**. Every event should
*cause* the next one.

### Level 2: Big beats. Save the Cat! (15 beats)
From *Save the Cat! Writes a Novel* (Jessica Brody, based on Blake Snyder). The
percentages show roughly where each beat lands. They're guidelines, not rules.
([Brody](https://www.jessicabrody.com/2020/11/how-to-write-your-novel-using-the-save-the-cat-beat-sheet/),
[Reedsy](https://reedsy.com/live/save-the-cat-writes-a-novel/))

| # | Beat | ~Where | What happens |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Opening Image | 0–1% | A snapshot of the hero's life *before* (shows the Lie) |
| 2 | Theme Stated | ~5% | Someone hints at the lesson the hero needs |
| 3 | Setup | 1–10% | The world, the hero's flaws, what's missing |
| 4 | Catalyst | ~10% | The event that changes everything |
| 5 | Debate | 10–20% | Hero hesitates: should I go? |
| 6 | Break into Two | ~20% | Hero commits and enters a new world or situation |
| 7 | B Story | ~22% | A new relationship that will teach the theme (often the knight–wizard bond) |
| 8 | Fun and Games | 20–50% | The "promise of the premise," the fun part readers came for |
| 9 | Midpoint | ~50% | A false victory or false defeat; stakes rise |
| 10 | Bad Guys Close In | 50–75% | Outside enemies *and* inner flaws squeeze tighter |
| 11 | All Is Lost | ~75% | The worst moment, often a loss or death |
| 12 | Dark Night of the Soul | 75–80% | Hero hits bottom, then realizes the Truth |
| 13 | Break into Three | ~80% | A new plan that uses the Truth |
| 14 | Finale | 80–99% | Hero acts on the Truth and wins (or loses) |
| 15 | Final Image | 99–100% | Mirror of the Opening Image, showing the change |

### Level 2 (alternative): The Hero's Journey
Joseph Campbell described the pattern in *The Hero with a Thousand Faces*
(1949). Christopher Vogler condensed it into 12 stages: Ordinary World → Call to
Adventure → Refusal → **Meeting the Mentor** → Crossing the Threshold → Tests,
Allies, Enemies → Approach → Ordeal → Reward → Road Back → Resurrection → Return
with the Elixir.
([Final Draft](https://www.finaldraft.com/blog/breaking-down-the-character-archetypes-of-the-heros-journey))
The **wizard-as-mentor** fills the "Meeting the Mentor" stage, which is why it's
so common (and also a cliché; see Lesson 12). Critics say the pattern gets
applied too rigidly, so use it as a lens, not a formula.

### Level 3: Scenes (Goal → Conflict → Disaster, then Reaction → Dilemma → Decision)
From Dwight Swain's *Techniques of the Selling Writer*.
([TCK Publishing](https://www.tckpublishing.com/scene-and-sequel/),
[Randy Ingermanson](https://www.advancedfictionwriting.com/?p=75))

- **Scene (action):** The character has a **Goal**, meets **Conflict**, and
  ends in a **Disaster** (they fail, or win at a cost).
- **Sequel (reaction):** **Reaction** (emotion) → **Dilemma** (bad options) →
  **Decision**. The decision becomes the next scene's goal.
- More scenes means faster pacing. More sequels means deeper emotion.

**Exercise 9.** Write your Part 1 as a Story Spine (8 lines). Then place your
Catalyst, Midpoint, All Is Lost, and Finale on the Save the Cat table.

## Lesson 10 — Short Stories and Multi-Part Series

**What it is.** Writing short pieces that are each satisfying on their own and
still make readers want the next part.

### Length categories (SFWA / Nebula Award definitions)
([Reedsy](https://blog.reedsy.com/how-long-is-a-short-story),
[The Novelry](https://www.thenovelry.com/blog/what-is-a-novella))

| Type | Words | Roughly | Best use for you |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short story | under 7,500 | 15–30 pages | free newsletter bonus, prequel, kids' story |
| Novelette | 7,500–17,500 | 30–70 pages | short "episodes" |
| **Novella** | **17,500–40,000** | **70–160 pages** | **The sweet spot for a multi-part series** |
| Novel | 40,000+ | 160+ pages | omnibus (several parts combined) |

### The MICE Quotient: the key to multi-part stories
Created by Orson Scott Card and expanded by Mary Robinette Kowal on *Writing
Excuses*. Every story is made of **threads**, and each thread **opens with a
question and closes when that question is answered**.
([Writing Excuses](https://writingexcuses.com/16-35-what-is-the-m-i-c-e-quotient/),
[TCK Publishing](https://www.tckpublishing.com/mice-quotient/))

| Thread | Opens when… | Closes when… | Wizards & knights example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **M**ilieu (place) | a character enters a place | they leave it (or decide to stay) | entering the Forbidden Library |
| **I**nquiry (question) | a question is raised | it's answered | *Who poisoned the Grand Master?* |
| **C**haracter (identity) | a character is unhappy with who they are | they change (or accept themselves) | *Am I a real knight without my sword hand?* |
| **E**vent (disruption) | the world's order is broken | order is restored (or a new one forms) | *A dragon wakes; the kingdom is under threat.* |

**Nesting:** Threads close in the **reverse order** they opened (first in, last
out), like nesting dolls. Kowal also teaches that **each thread you open adds
length**. To keep a story short, open fewer threads.
([Writers Atelier](https://writersatelier.com/?p=8035))

### How to make a multi-part series work
1. **Each part closes at least one thread completely.** That's what makes it
   *satisfying*.
2. **Each part leaves the big outer thread open.** That's what makes readers
   *buy the next one*.
3. Think of it like TV: each part has an **episode plot** (closed) and moves
   the **season plot** forward (still open).
4. **End on a new question, not halfway through the action.** *(My advice.)*
   Readers who paid for a part and feel cheated by an unresolved cliffhanger
   leave angry reviews. Make it a "hook," not a "cut-off."

**Three series formats:**

| Format | How it works | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Serial** | One big story split into parts | Strong pull to keep reading | Readers must start at Part 1 |
| **Episodic** | Same heroes, a standalone adventure each time | Readers can start anywhere; great for kids | Weaker pull to the next book |
| **Hybrid** *(recommended)* | Standalone adventure in each part, plus a season arc in the background | Best of both | Takes more planning |

> **Note:** Amazon's serial platform, **Kindle Vella, shut down in February
> 2025.** ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2024/amazon-to-shut-down-kindle-vella-serial-book-platform-saying-it-hasnt-caught-on-as-wed-hoped/))
> On Amazon today, "multi-part" means separate ebooks linked on a **series
> page**. Some authors post chapters free on Royal Road, sell early access on
> Patreon, and then publish on Amazon. That approach can conflict with Kindle
> Unlimited's exclusivity rules.
> ([ScribeCount](https://scribecount.com/author-resource/publishing-wide/serialized-fiction-platforms))

**Exercise 10.** List your series' outer thread (E, I, M, or C). Then list
one inner thread that Part 1 opens *and closes*.

## Lesson 11 — Point of View and Voice

**What it is.** *Point of view* is whose eyes the reader experiences the story
through. *Voice* is how the narration sounds.

| POV | Feels like | Common in |
|---|---|---|
| **First person** ("I drew my sword") | Very close and intimate; fast | YA, often in the **present tense** |
| **Third person limited** ("She drew her sword") | Close to one character per scene, with room to pull back | Adult fantasy, multi-POV series |
| **Third omniscient** (all-knowing narrator) | Storyteller voice | Classic fairy tales, some kids' books |

([Goodreads author blogs on POV in YA](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/9110870-pov-choices-in-ya))

**Rules of thumb:**
- **One point of view per scene.** Switch only at a scene break.
- A **knight-and-wizard duo** works well with **alternating third-person
  limited**: knight chapter, wizard chapter. Readers see both sides of the
  relationship.
- Check recent bestsellers in *your* category and match what readers expect.
- **Read your pages aloud.** That's the fastest way to hear your voice and
  catch awkward lines.

## Lesson 12 — Tropes (Use Them on Purpose)

**What it is.** Tropes are familiar story patterns: the chosen one, the wise
old wizard, the farm boy with a secret destiny, the knight in shining armor.

**Why it matters.** Readers *like* tropes because they're part of the genre's
promise. Too many make a book predictable, and none at all can lose genre
readers. ([MasterClass](https://www.masterclass.com/articles/how-to-use-fantasy-tropes-effectively))

**How to do it:** Keep the trope's *shape* but **twist one thing**: the
motivation, the character's willingness, or the consequence.
([Writers Write](https://www.writerswrite.co.za/?p=1241427),
[Jennifer Oliver](https://jenniferoliverwriter.substack.com/p/subverted-tropes-deconstructing-fantasy))

| Trope | One twist |
|---|---|
| Wise old wizard mentor | The mentor is young, or selfish, or **wrong** |
| Chosen one | The chosen one **refuses** and walks away, and someone else steps up |
| Farm boy with a destiny | Nobody believes him, or the prophecy was about someone else |
| Knight in shining armor | The knight's code is corrupt, or the shining armor hides fear |
| Dark lord | The "dark lord" is the only one telling the truth |

**Know your subgenre's promise.** These are current market signals from 2026
trend reports
([Author Media](https://www.authormedia.com/zeitgeist-cozy-fantasy-and-litrpg-surge-as-hottest-niches-in-2026/),
[PublishDrive](https://publishdrive.com/romantasy-book-trends.html)). Treat
them as directional; they come from industry blogs, not official sales data.
- **Romantasy** (romance and fantasy combined) has driven much of fantasy's
  recent growth. *Adult audience.*
- **Cozy fantasy**, **LitRPG**, and **YA dark fantasy** are named as niches
  where reader demand is growing faster than the supply of books.
- Readers of each subgenre expect certain things. **Cozy** means low stakes and
  comfort. **Romantasy** means a central romance with a happy ending. **Epic**
  means big battles and world-shaking stakes.

## Lesson 13 — The Writing Process and Revision

**Plotter or pantser?** "Plotters" outline first. "Pantsers" (also called
"discovery writers" or "gardeners") find the story as they write. Both work.
For a first series, I recommend **a light outline**: your Story Spine plus 15
beats, then discovery inside each chapter. Multi-part series need *some*
planning so the season arc pays off.

**Build a habit with simple math:**
- 500 words a day is about **50 days** for a 25,000-word novella.
- 1,000 words a day is about **25 days**.
- Writing at the same time every day beats waiting for inspiration.

**Draft fast, fix later.** A first draft is supposed to be messy. Don't edit
chapter 1 again until the whole draft is done.

**Revise in layers, big to small:**
1. **Story:** Does the plot make sense? Does the arc land? Any plot holes?
2. **Scenes:** Does every scene have a goal, a conflict, and a turn? Cut any
   that don't.
3. **Lines:** Prose, dialogue, and "shown" emotion (Lesson 8). Read it aloud.
4. **Proofread:** Spelling, grammar, and formatting.

**Beta readers:** Before paying an editor, give the book to 3–5 readers *in your
target audience*. For a kids' book, that means kids *and* their parents or
teachers. Ask them where they got bored, where they got confused, and what they
loved.

**Then editing.** Costs and options are in
[`03-publishing-and-money.md`](03-publishing-and-money.md#step-3--editing).
